Alex, you're a genuinely exceptional player. Rest in that. Trust that those who value the actual over the over-polished will see you for who you are: a phenomenal musician, an improviser, a highly skilled guitarist, and someone who has clearly poured their life's blood into developing the level you've reached.

There's nothing wrong with highly edited work—some of it is beautiful from a compositional standpoint. But part of what you're battling is the perception gap between people who understand the difference between real-life, unedited playing and studio-crafted perfection and those who don't. Many chase spectacle over substance, or worse, hunt for a single mistake so they can diminish someone else's achievement in their own mind. A lot of players cater to that market, often for reasons that have nothing to do with integrity.

The idea that someone at your level couldn't edit together a flawless take whenever they wanted is absurd. That's not the issue. It comes down to who you are and what you want to deliver. Context is king.

I listen to you because I hear you in there—your touch, your musicality, your ideas. I respect what you've built and what you create. I love polished (highly edited) work when it's honest about its own process, but respect is earned in this context through the actual ability to play the instrument in real time, not through editing those performances.

When I hear you, what I hear is an improviser—someone with great skill who loves music, loves the guitar, and needs to express themselves through those pathways. And the reason you're wrestling with this dilemma is that you have integrity. You are concerned with presenting your playing as it is, and I love that! You're thinking in terms of improvisation, authenticity, and presenting the level you actually play at, not the level that can be assembled through DAW edits and sound design, not that those are wrong or less than; they change the context, which in a world that values integrity would just be an open thing for all to see.

I wish more players were as transparent. When musicians set standards that no physical instrument can meet without editing, that's fine—but that context should be stated plainly. Then the lines are clear, and everyone can understand what they're hearing. Otherwise, we lose the critical distinctions and misplace our valuations, which can harm players, listeners, and budding musicians who have misplaced ideas about what composition and improvisation share and what they differ in, and this can cause significant psychological distress and harm to both their lives and business/career outcomes.

I love the path you've chosen. Your music and improvisations are what drew me to you in the first place. If players like you stop presenting naked, real-time music—with all the highs and lows inherent to improvisation—the world loses something far more valuable than whatever it thinks it gains from hyper-edited content.

The time, discipline, tenacity, and intelligence it takes to play at your level can't be taken away, even if society, for a moment, misplaces its appreciation for it. And the difference between repeating the same notes every night and improvising within a performance is not about superiority—it's about context. Some can do neither. Some rehearse and execute flawlessly. Some rehearse, execute, and still improvise new life into the composition every time they take a solo.

My job as a listener is to understand what I'm hearing and let the music speak for itself. Taste is beside the point.

What is key is this: you're standing in the middle of a societal tension—between presenting yourself in a way that gathers disproportionate praise, likes, and clicks, which often equals money, but for things that have not been presented in the open light of truth (that is why it is disproportionate). Why is it this way? They want the fast, easier path, and they decide to gain what they have not earned through the Conference of the Unobtained. In many cases, those composers want the street cred of being seen as high-level guitarists (honestly, next level otherworldly guitarists) versus presenting themselves as they are, which is often composers, great editors, and compilers of sounds they love while having some good guitar skills. So, here you stand, and you are right to present this, because there is something wrong here that often goes unaddressed in the open, though it is happening more often. When people want the credit for things that don't align with what they are actually doing, and that credit changes the perception of the listening public in ways that alter the actual vs. the idealized, it conveys real harm to those who actually do those things that others claim to be doing. You are doing! You can play the hell out of your instrument! You can improvise and make moments happen, and this is important. Your playing is inspiring as a result of investing in these things and the road less traveled.

In the end, you have already achieved so much, and anyone who knows what to look for will see your great work. I know the problem is that those who don't know, and the lies that encourage them to keep from learning, may not find you or understand when they do, but the more players that speak out and take a stand for transparency, the better things will get. As that improves, you will undoubtedly be seen more clearly by all instead of some. If players become more honest about the context, things could get better for everyone. There will be ebb and flow, and I pray we take a step back toward a more informed environment sooner than later.

I hope you keep choosing truth. You are killing it, and my life has been enhanced by you, your playing, and the things you have poured your heart and soul into developing. Keep going, Alex! Keep the transparency and make the music you love through whatever good means that help you make it! Thank you for all you do. Here is to much more success for you throughout your life.

Everyone's judgement on it is different of course, but in my experience, whether or not you value authentic, live performance, warts and all, reflects hugely on your approach to music in general. I think it reflects who you are as a musician; whether you value just the appearance of the end product or you value the whole experience of creating genuine art. I think this shows through in the types of artists who are more likely to publish unedited performances, either just on youtube or on albums. In my experience, artists who are happy to publish genuine, imperfect, human performances usually make "better art" (or at least they share my philosophy of art so I think of it as better), as compared to artists who piece everything together in a very artificial way. I think it's somewhat similar to vocalists who use pitch correction. At the end of the day it's just a tool, but your willingness to use that tool reflects on your overall approach and philosophy towards the art form.
